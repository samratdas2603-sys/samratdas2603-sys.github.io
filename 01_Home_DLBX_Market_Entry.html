Market entry strategy, Ireland and the UK

The bigger market was the smaller opportunity

Home DLBX was preparing to launch a digital building logbook and had to choose where to start. England builds five to six times more homes than Ireland. I recommended Ireland, because what mattered was not how much gets built, but how much the law requires to be documented.

The brief

The question behind the question

The founder asked which market to enter first. But Home DLBX does not sell into a construction market. It sells to organisations that are legally required to keep building records, and those duties are written very differently on each side of the Irish Sea.

So the real question was not which country builds more homes. It was which country compels more documentation, and in what shape.

Finding one

Two legal duties, two different shapes

Ireland's building control rules require certified records on about 84% of new homes, roughly 30,000 units a year, and that pool refills every year. The UK's golden thread duty is stricter about digital records, but it covers only higher-risk buildings: a fixed population of about 12,500 that does not renew.

Over one year the gap looks large. Over five, it compounds.

A duty that renews every year suits a new entrant. A fixed pool tends to settle on one default provider early.

That asymmetry drove the recommendation more than any score did. It also set the clock: the UK's pool is finite, so waiting is not free.

Finding two

Nine organisations hold most of the market

Ireland has 438 Approved Housing Bodies, but nine of them hold 73% of the sector's homes, and 82% of forecast growth sits with 2% of bodies. For a company with no sales team, an abstract market becomes a list of nine conversations.

The correction

Where I was wrong

I began assuming Ireland was competitively open, and that the platform would mainly be displacing spreadsheets and shared drives. The evidence said otherwise.

An established competitor had more than 160 Irish customers, a new Dublin office and a product built for the exact segment I was about to recommend. I lowered Ireland's competitive score and wrote the change into the client report, rather than quietly adjusting the conclusion.

Its filed accounts then added nuance. Cumulative losses, reliance on its parent company and no reported R&D spend pointed to strength in an installed customer base, not active product investment. That shaped the positioning: compete on what happens after a building is handed over, where the competitor has nothing, rather than head-on.

Positioning

Compete after handover, not at it

The competitor's four product families all sit in construction and handover, used by professionals. What happens to a building's records after that, when owners live in it, retrofit it and sell it, is largely uncovered. That is also where the next wave of regulation lands.

The recommendation

Ireland first, with the UK running from day one

Enter through Approved Housing Bodies' new-build pipelines, where there is no incumbent supplier to displace. Start UK groundwork immediately rather than after, because the UK's mandated pool is finite and already consolidating.

Three phases, three decision points

Indicative sequence, not fixed dates

Phase 1

Establish in Ireland

Two or three reference sites with housing bodies on new-build schemes. Early UK relationships and regulatory tracking alongside.

Phase 2

Scale in Ireland

Extend across the largest bodies and into developers. Decide the UK route, direct or through a partner.

Phase 3

Commit to the UK

Enter commercially, scoped to the mandated population as it then stands. Energy features against the EU timetable.

Before each phase: did the reference sites land on time, has UK regulation widened, and is the product ready to scale at low cost? Each is a moment to test the plan against evidence, not just follow it.

Outcome

Where it landed

The founder now has a sequenced entry plan: which market to enter first, which segment to enter through, what to compete on, and three decision points at which to test the plan against evidence rather than simply follow it. I presented the findings to her directly, and she asked for the full research report to take forward.

Open questions

What the analysis could not settle

  1. Will buyers pay? The analysis rests on legal obligation, not purchasing intent. Willingness to pay is untested until the first reference sites are signed.
  2. Will the UK widen its register? A Remediation Bill has been announced but not introduced. If it extends registration to buildings between 11 and 18 metres, the UK case strengthens.
  3. When will self-scanning be ready? Until 3D capture is in-house, each dwelling carries an outsourced scanning cost that shapes how fast the UK can scale.

Looking back

What I would do differently

I would test the assumption that Ireland was uncontested in the first week, rather than discovering the competitor halfway through. The analysis was stronger for the correction, but looking for disconfirming evidence early would have been faster and no less honest.
