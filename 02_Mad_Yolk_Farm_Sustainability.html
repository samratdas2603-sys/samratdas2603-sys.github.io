Short on hours, not money

A free-range egg farm in East Galway wanted external recognition for its sustainability work, but could not afford to chase the wrong scheme or stall halfway through one. We compared four accreditations, then built a toolkit around the constraint that actually binds a small farm: time.

The brief

A good farm with no way to prove it

Mad Yolk runs free-range hens on 33 acres, alongside a market garden, an honesty farm shop and a community sauna. The owner had planted around 12,000 to 13,000 trees in five years and installed solar panels that cut electricity costs by about 40%.

None of it was measured or documented. Decisions were made by instinct, which left the farm unable to evidence its own record, and exposed to funders, buyers and accreditation bodies that increasingly ask for numbers.

Finding one

Four schemes, four different jobs

We found no scheme that is simply best. Each serves a different strategic purpose, so the right choice depends on what the business most needs the label to do.

Finding two

The binding constraint was time

Owner-managers of small firms are usually shorter of hours than of capital. So we designed every part of the toolkit around available time rather than budget, and pushed the free work to the front.

The roadmap

Twelve months, six stages, a decision at every gate

The roadmap borrows the plan, do, check, act cycle at the heart of ISO 14001. Any stage can pause at its gate without losing work, which matters for a business that may need to stop during a busy season.

Why this order: a baseline comes before any policy, because a policy written without data produces aspirations rather than auditable actions. A mock audit is built into stage 5, because first audits usually fail on missing documents rather than performance.

The farm

Strong instincts, thin evidence

Applying the toolkit to Mad Yolk showed a farm whose practice was ahead of its paperwork, with one structural exposure it could not ignore.

Strengths

  • 12,000 to 13,000 trees planted in five years for biodiversity and soil
  • About 40% lower electricity costs after solar went in late 2025
  • Diversified income from eggs, farm shop, sauna and community events

Gaps

  • Imported soya feed, the largest emissions line and the largest supply risk
  • No structured policy or tracking, so real achievements go unrecorded
  • Water security resting on a borehole and storage not yet fully developed

Recommendations

Three moves, in order

1

Measure first

Everything else depends on data, so the first step is a quarterly dashboard across energy, water, biodiversity and community, built from records the farm already has. It needs about two hours a month from one part-time person, and turns the owner's aim of persuading neighbours with numbers rather than argument into something he can actually do.

2

Replace imported soya in phases

Soya meal supplies roughly 55 to 65% of the protein in a typical European laying diet. A sudden switch risks production, so we proposed three steps, each tracked on the dashboard.

Years 1–2Replace 10–20% with Irish-grown peas and faba beans, rebalancing amino acids
Years 2–4Assess black soldier fly meal, authorised for poultry feed since 2017
Year 3 onwardsMove towards ducks, around 20% feed and 80% pasture
3

Turn community goodwill into steady income

Customers and the local community are the farm's most important stakeholders. We proposed a community-supported agriculture membership, paid quarterly in advance, which moves some production risk to members and gives the farm working capital to expand its solar.

Basic: eggs and vegetablesStandard: plus sauna and visitsPatron: plus event access

At 50 to 100 members, that could bring in roughly €30,000 to €90,000 a year, benchmarked against an Irish community farm supporting a salaried manager from about 90 member families.

Outcome

Where it landed

The farm received a management briefing comparing the four accreditation routes, a toolkit it can run without outside help, with seven templates and a twelve-month roadmap, and a case study with three prioritised recommendations. Because the toolkit is sector-transferable, other small agri-food businesses can reuse it.

Open questions

What the analysis could not settle

  1. Can the owner protect four hours a week? The readiness check makes this the first gate, and it is the most likely point of failure for a busy one-person operation.
  2. How will the flock perform on less soya? The first substitution step is deliberately small, so production can be tracked on the dashboard before going further.
  3. Is there local demand for 50 members? The membership income range depends on it, so a small pilot tier would test demand cheaply before a full launch.
