Protocol

How the question was made searchable

The review was framed with an adapted PICO structure, then turned into one Boolean string run on titles, abstracts and keywords. Peer-reviewed English-language journal articles only; quality was appraised with criteria adapted from the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool.

P · POPULATIONGlobal supply chains and firms in international production networks
I · INTERESTTrade wars, armed conflict, export controls, sanctions, regional crises
C · COMPARISONSupply chains exposed to equivalent geopolitical stress
O · OUTCOMEChanges in architecture, resilience, performance and governance
("geopolitical" OR "trade war" OR "sanctions" OR "conflict")
AND ("supply chain" OR "global value chain" OR "logistics")
AND ("disruption" OR "resilience" OR "restructuring" OR "reshoring")

Synthesis followed Thomas and Harden's three stages: line-by-line coding, descriptive themes, then analytical themes that answer the question.

Findings

Four themes

1

Ripple effects spread disruption

Geopolitical shocks change the structure of a supply network, not just its stock levels. Lean, concentrated sourcing speeds the spread: firms with highly concentrated sourcing took disproportionate output losses when tariffs arrived.

2

Resilience pays most when built in advance

Firms combining redundancy (dual sourcing, buffer stock) with flexibility (postponement, adaptive planning) recover faster than firms relying on either one alone.

3

Redesign: regionalising and reshoring

Temporary workarounds harden into permanent structure. Rerouted corridors attract investment and stay, and export controls push targeted countries to build their own capability.

4

Governance moderates the outcome

Boards that build geopolitical risk into scenario planning and supplier diversification recover faster. Modular value chains, with capable and diverse suppliers, switch partners more easily than captive ones.

Conceptual framework

Disruption, Response, Reconfiguration

The group combined the four themes into a three-layer model of how a geopolitical shock moves through a supply chain over time.

Layer 1

Disruption

Shocks enter through trade policy, armed conflict, maritime threats and regulatory fragmentation, amplified by lean and concentrated networks.

Layer 2

Response

Operational moves (buffers, dual sourcing, rerouting) and strategic ones (redesign, switching, upgrading), shaped by governance and policy.

Layer 3

Reconfiguration

Lasting changes in geography and governance that become the starting point for the next shock.

Feedback loop: reconfiguration reshapes the institutional landscape, which changes how the next disruption lands.

Conclusion

Geopolitical risk is now a first-order strategic variable

Supply chains designed only for efficiency in stable conditions are systematically exposed. The practical implications:

  • Invest before the shock. Dual sourcing, regional backup capacity and strategic inventory cost less and protect continuity better than emergency response.
  • Some redundancy is a feature. Moderate decentralisation reduced network vulnerability in the evidence, even though it costs more in calm periods.
  • Know your switching options in advance. Building intelligence on alternative suppliers and routes before a disruption is what makes switching possible when it is needed.
  • Put geopolitics on the board agenda. Scenario planning and institutionalised supplier diversification are linked to faster recovery.
