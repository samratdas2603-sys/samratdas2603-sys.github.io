Independent strategy implementation analysis

The plan was sound. The structure would stall it.

easyJet's implementation plan set out three initiatives, each cutting across several departments at once. I assessed whether the organisation could actually deliver them, and found the biggest risk was not in the plan but in how the company was organised to carry it out.

The question

Can the organisation actually deliver it?

The plan aimed to extend easyJet's reach, sharpen its operations and keep more customers, without giving up its low-cost model. Most strategy work stops at whether a plan is right. I looked at whether the company was set up to execute it: the context, the structure, the politics and the leadership each initiative would need.

The change context

Feasible, if resourced and sequenced

I tested the plan against seven factors that shape how change should be designed. Most were workable. Two were real concerns, and both came from running all three initiatives at once.

The central finding

Three initiatives, and every one crosses departments

Under a traditional hierarchy, every cross-department decision has to travel up to senior management and back down. With three initiatives running at once, that becomes the bottleneck. I recommended a matrix structure instead.

How each structure carries the plan

Switch between the two

  • Slow decisionsApprovals move up and down management layers, delaying alignment across operations, IT, commercial and finance.
  • Departmental goals winManagers optimise their own KPIs rather than the three initiatives together, fragmenting execution.
  • Resources driftWhen projects compete for the same people, they flow to whichever department holds the most power.
  • Clear ownershipEach initiative gets a dedicated lead, with people drawn from every function it needs.
  • Expertise keptStaff stay in their functions, so specialist knowledge is not lost to the projects.
  • Rebalancing at the topSenior management can move people and budget between the three streams as priorities shift.

Functions involved per initiative follow the implementation plan: the codeshare spans network planning, revenue, IT and customer teams; Skywise spans operations, engineering and finance; the loyalty platform spans IT, marketing and customer operations.

Making it stick

A deliberate plan, with room to learn

The plan was a classic deliberate strategy: set objectives, timelines and budgets from the top. That gave it structure, but little room to learn as it ran. I proposed adding emergent elements, plus four political levers to manage resistance.

Three adjustments to the plan

  1. Quarterly feedback loops after each AI phase, so the rollout adapts to real results.
  2. A cross-functional learning team recording what each initiative teaches the others.
  3. Local flexibility for regional managers on codeshare ground procedures.

Four political levers

  1. Resource control: priority training access for early adopters of AI tools.
  2. Alliances: respected senior pilots and operations managers as internal advisers.
  3. Timing: launching the AI pilot when operational performance is strong.
  4. Symbolic action: leaders visibly using the new tools, including flying the first codeshare service.

Leadership and communication

Advocate the why, then ask before assuming

I recommended transformational leadership, with executives modelling the change rather than just announcing it. For each staff group, the plan pairs advocacy, explaining why the change matters, with inquiry, asking what the change looks like from where they sit.

Operations staff

Advocacy

Why AI is needed: competitive pressure, efficiency and safety.

Inquiry

What concerns do you have about AI-driven scheduling?

IT teams

Advocacy

Why all three matter, and how their workload will be prioritised.

Inquiry

What resources would make this timeline achievable?

Customer service

Advocacy

How self-service removes repetitive queries and frees staff for better work.

Inquiry

Which customer pain points should the platform solve first?

Without this, silence fills with rumours about job losses, problems surface late, and frontline solutions never reach decision-makers.

Outcome

Where it landed

The analysis judged the plan feasible if properly resourced and carefully sequenced, and turned that into specific actions: evolutionary rather than revolutionary change, a matrix structure with a dedicated lead for each initiative, three adjustments to let a deliberate plan learn, and a communication plan for each staff group.

Open questions

What the analysis could not settle

  1. Can shared IT carry all three at once? Every initiative depends on the same IT teams. If capacity falls short, one initiative may need to start later.
  2. How will crews and unions respond to AI-assisted decisions? Cultural readiness was the weakest part of the context, and it could slow the Skywise rollout.
  3. Does a codeshare fit a point-to-point model? It is the largest strategic departure of the three, and its commercial return is the least tested.
